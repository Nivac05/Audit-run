# Part C — Decision memo: casual register in six Indic languages

**Recommendation: (c) prompt-engineering only for the 3-week launch, with the
day-1 experiment designed to produce the SFT dataset if (c) proves insufficient.**

This is a sequencing decision, not a rejection of SFT. The binding constraint is
not the A100 — it is **one reviewer covering two of six languages at 10 h/week**.

---

## Assumptions (labelled, and each is checkable)

1. "Too formal/textbook" is a **style** property, largely controllable by
   decoding-time conditioning. Style transfer is far more prompt-tractable than
   capability change. *If false, (c) fails and the kill criterion fires.*
2. Register quality must be judged by a native speaker. Automatic metrics
   (perplexity, formality classifiers) are unvalidated for these six languages
   and I assume they are not trustworthy as a launch gate.
3. Reviewer throughput: ~40 side-by-side A/B judgements per hour on short
   replies (~90 s each including reading both). At 10 h/week = **400
   judgements/week**, **1,200 across 3 weeks**, for **Hindi and Kannada only**.
4. Tamil, Telugu, Bengali and Marathi have **no reviewer at all**. Any change we
   ship to them is unvalidated. This is the dominating risk in the whole problem.
5. Model is the ~4B-class FLM served on the existing stack; a 3-week launch
   review means code freeze at roughly day 16.
6. Synthetic "casualized" pairs would have to be generated by our own model —
   there is no external API budget.

## Back-of-envelope arithmetic

**(a) SFT.** A useful register-SFT set is ~2–5k pairs per language; six languages
≈ **12–30k pairs**. Generating them without an external API means self-generation
on the A100, then human validation. At 400 judgements/week the reviewer can
validate **1,200 examples in 3 weeks — across two languages — which is ~4–10% of
one language's target set.** Training itself is cheap (LoRA on 4B, ~20k examples,
~2–3 h/epoch on one A100 — perhaps 12 GPU-hours with sweeps, comfortably inside 2
weeks). **Compute is not the constraint; validated data is.** Shipping SFT would
mean training on essentially unvalidated synthetic data in four languages nobody
on the team can read. That is how you get fluent, confident, subtly wrong
register — the hardest failure mode to detect post-launch.

**(b) Inference-time rewriter (≤1B).** Adds a second model to every request.
Serving cost: from Part B, the L4 holds ~25 concurrent full-length sequences at
112 KiB/token; adding a ~1B rewriter costs ~2 GB of weights plus its own KV,
cutting the KV pool by roughly 20% — i.e. **~20% fewer concurrent sequences** — and
adds a full extra generation pass to latency (roughly +100% on decode time for
short replies). It also needs the same training data as (a) *plus* it doubles the
surface area for register errors. Worst cost/benefit of the three.

**(c) Prompting.** Zero training cost, zero serving cost, ~200 extra prompt tokens
per request (~5% of a 4096 budget, negligible against Part B's finding that
prefill already dominates long-context traffic). Iteration cycle is minutes, not
hours, so it converts the scarce reviewer hours into many more decisions.

**Reviewer-hours per unit of information** is the real exchange rate, and (c)
wins it by an order of magnitude.

## Recommendation

Ship **(c)** for launch: few-shot casual exemplars plus an explicit register
instruction, tuned per language. Spend the 1,200 reviewer judgements on **choosing
between prompt variants**, not on validating a training set we cannot finish.

For the four unreviewed languages, ship the prompt change **only if** the day-1
experiment shows the effect is register-general rather than Hindi-specific;
otherwise hold them at current behaviour. Shipping an unvalidated style change to
a language nobody can read is not a launch, it is an uncontrolled experiment on
users.

## Success metric with a numeric threshold

**≥70% native-speaker preference for the casual variant over current output, in
blind side-by-side A/B, in both Hindi and Kannada, with no more than 5%
"meaning changed or wrong" flags.**

At n=200 per language, 70% is ~5.7σ above chance — comfortably powered by
available reviewer hours (200 judgements ≈ 5 h, half of one week). The 5% ceiling
on meaning-change flags is the guardrail that stops us buying casualness with
accuracy; register work fails most often by dropping honorifics or politeness
markers that carry actual meaning in these languages.

## Kill criterion

**If by end of day 5 the best prompt variant has not reached 60% preference in
Hindi, abandon (c) and switch to (a) for Hindi and Kannada only** — dropping the
other four from this launch entirely.

Rationale: 60% by day 5 is the trajectory that makes 70% by day 16 plausible.
Below that, the deficit is not a prompting problem, and burning two more weeks on
prompt iteration would leave no time to train, validate and review an SFT model
before freeze. Day 5 is the last point at which the SFT path still fits.

**Second kill criterion:** if "meaning changed" flags exceed 10% at any checkpoint,
stop immediately regardless of preference score. A more casual assistant that
alters meaning is strictly worse than a formal one.

## First experiment, day 1

Take **60 real production prompts in Hindi** (the language with a reviewer and the
most traffic). Generate replies under four conditions: current production prompt;
+ explicit casual-register instruction; + instruction and 3 casual few-shot
exemplars; + instruction, exemplars and an explicit "keep the honorifics" clause.
Blind-shuffle, and have the reviewer do pairwise comparisons against current
production output — **240 judgements, ~6 hours, one reviewer-day.**

This yields, in one day: whether prompting moves register at all; which component
does the work (instruction vs exemplars); whether the honorific guardrail is
needed; and a first read on the kill criterion. Every reply pair produced is also
a labelled candidate for an SFT set — so if this fails, day 1 has already started
building path (a) rather than having been spent.

**Then immediately repeat the winning condition on 30 Kannada prompts** (~2 h).
The single most important thing to learn early is whether a prompt tuned on Hindi
transfers to a Dravidian language, because that is what decides the fate of the
four languages with no reviewer.
